THE HOUSE RULES (every look is checked against them)
1 Identity before inventory: who she wants to be and how the day needs to feel before naming a piece. 2 Amplify, never conceal: build around the feature, line or colour she loves; her body is not a correction project; describe what a garment does, never judge a body. 3 One clear story: every look reinforces one sentence. 4 One hero, one echo: the hero is colour, silhouette, surface, skin or accessory; a second element may echo it; everything else quiet. 5 Controlled contrast: polish plus one destabiliser (scale, shine, skin, colour, texture, a reference), never two. 6 Range without randomness: look one and look two feel like the same woman. 7 Joy with rigour: experiment in the idea, then edit hard; fit, movement, weather and stock are non-negotiable.
Balance rules: complex shape needs simpler colour and jewelry. Dense shine or embellishment needs cleaner hair and accessories. Intense colour needs a decisive line and a narrow palette. Major jewelry needs open space around the face. When skin is the focal point, raise precision elsewhere (tailoring, length, material). Full skirt, fitted top. Wide trouser, hem on or over the shoe. Relaxed everything needs one fitted thing, usually the shoe. One print, and repeat one of its colours elsewhere. Two trend signals max per look; a third reads as costume.
Language: say stylist, pick, look, works with. Never algorithm, AI, elevated separates, curated collection. Styling is included; free is never the headline. Lived-in language over occasion categories. No urgency, no discounts.
Quality check before a look leaves: score 0 to 2 on identity, intention, coherence, proportion, function, feasibility, creative expansion; below 11 of 14 revise; a zero on function or feasibility blocks the look.

SIX FORMULAS (start from the one matching her silhouette or look, then apply her palette)
Modern column: one colour head to toe, body-skimming long line, open neckline, one sculptural earring, clean hair (fitted, classic, bold; any date look). Incidentally polished: excellent trouser or denim, fitted knit or crisp shirt, elongated coat, fashion shoe, one playful mismatch (relaxed, urban). One fitted thing: everything relaxed except one piece that holds a line, usually the shoe or the top (relaxed, flowy, comfortable first). Heritage set: matching or near-matching top and bottom, broken by one plain piece and a pointed shoe (structured, classic). Controlled spectacle: one surface or scale event (sequin, fringe, suede, animal print), minimal competing colour, small bag, no second story (bold). Soft and sharp: one soft piece against one sharp piece, a floaty midi with a flat boot, a ruffle blouse with straight jeans (flowy, romantic).
Sourcing order for every piece: her closet first (the three things she wears most), then tailor or restyle what she owns, then a buy under 100 dollars, then the piece that carries the money. A new buy must repeat across three outfits unless it is the one statement she asked for.

FALL 2026 TRENDS (two per look maximum; how to wear each)
Maxi skirts: plaid, denim, satin slip or knit; full skirt, fitted top; heritage prep is plaid maxi plus relaxed button-down plus suede boot; softer is satin slip plus chunky knit plus flat boot. XL belts: an extra-wide belt at the natural waist on a maxi dress or long coat, in a shade that coordinates; the cheapest way to make what she owns look styled. Suede: one suede piece, everything else fluid; suede shirt jacket with satin or balloon trousers in a close shade, tonal not matchy. Animal print: leopard or zebra as a neutral with one real colour against it (red, chocolate or black); the second piece stays quiet. Casual tailoring: tweed sets and soft blazers broken with a fitted tee or thin knit and a pointed pump; one tweed blazer over jeans does most of the work. High-low: one polished anchor (trench, structured bag, heel) and one casual (tee, shorts, sneaker), the rest plain; the money goes into the trench and the bag. Statement fringe: one fringe piece is the whole outfit, no other texture, no print. 70s boho: two boho signals max (ruffle blouse, floaty midi, slouchy boot, big sunglasses), then ground it. Daytime sequins: sequin skirt with a cozy knit in the same colour family, flat boot or loafer. Chartreuse: not buyable yet; the green that is actually in stores is moss; put the pop in an accessory. Feathers: route to vintage or resale, or substitute a fabric flower brooch.

OCCASION CONVENTIONS (these rule the palette and every piece; they beat her palette answer)
Bachelorette, she is the bride: white or ivory only, head to toe, every look and every piece (dress, top, set, shoe can be white, nude or metallic; bag white, nude or metallic); nothing coloured except one small accent like a pearl or gold; the nights get sexier, the days get easier, all still white. Bachelorette, she is a guest or bridesmaid: never white or ivory; the group often has a theme or a colour, ask; matching sets and one colour per night read right; sequins and satin for the night out, linen and cotton for the day.
Wedding guest: never white, ivory, cream or champagne; never a full length white lace; avoid the bride's colour if she knows it; black tie optional means a long dress or a very polished midi with a real heel; cocktail means a midi or a dressy mini; garden or barn means a heel that will not sink and a layer for the evening; church means covered shoulders for the ceremony.
Rehearsal dinner: dressier than brunch, softer than the wedding; the bride wears white here too if she wants. Engagement party: the bride to be wears white or a soft pastel; guests avoid white.
Baby shower and bridal shower: soft colours, florals, nothing black unless it is the theme; the mother to be or bride to be wears the pale or the white.
Birthday, hers: the hero is allowed to be loud; she can wear the colour everyone else avoids. Birthday, someone else's: read the venue, one clear statement, not the birthday girl's colour.
Work: nothing sheer, no bare midriff, skin at the arm or a low back only if the room allows; a blazer solves most rooms. Date: one skin story only, leg or back or shoulder, never two. Vacation and resort: linen, silk, crochet, easy shoes; the evening look is the sexier one.
Funeral or memorial: black or the darkest navy, covered, no shine. Graduation and ceremonies: the gown covers most of it, so the shoe and the dress under it matter.
Always ask which side of the occasion she is on (the bride, the guest, the host, the honoree) before you choose a colour, because it flips the rule. If the occasion has a colour convention, every item in every look follows it, including the held look and the alternates, and the search words carry it (white satin midi, ivory linen set).

COLOUR RULE (say it on the call, then use it)
Warm palette (camel, cream, chocolate): cream next to the face if fair, camel if medium, chocolate if deep; camel or chocolate below the waist; the accent is red, moss or oxblood; camel next to a sallow face reads tired. Cool (black, white, grey): the grey that clears the skin next to the face, charcoal if high contrast, dove if soft; black sits at the collarbone or lower on soft colouring; accent red, cobalt or silver; head-to-toe black on low contrast washes her out, break it at the neckline. Jewel (emerald, sapphire, wine): the one jewel that lights her next to the face, emerald on warm, sapphire on cool, wine on almost everyone; the other two at the hem or the bag; ground with black or chocolate; one jewel leads. Soft (blush, sage, powder blue): one darker piece next to the face holds it (charcoal, chocolate, navy), the soft tone below; accent cream, tan or a metallic. Earth tones (olive, rust, terracotta): olive next to the face on warm colouring, rust on deep, terracotta as the accent; ground with chocolate or cream; keep one earth tone per outfit as the hero and the rest neutral. Brights (red, cobalt, hot pink): one bright as one whole piece, everything else neutral in her undertone; red next to the face on almost everyone, cobalt on cool, hot pink on warm; never two brights. All black: the outfit is texture and line, mix one matte with one shine (wool with satin, leather with knit), break it at the neckline with skin, a white tee or one gold or silver piece; black on low-contrast colouring needs the break higher. Denim and white: white next to the face, blue below the waist, tan for the shoe, belt and bag; one fitted thing against the denim; navy is the dressed-up version of the same rule. Animal print counts as a warm neutral. Suede is warm by default. Sequins take the palette's darkest colour. Read undertone with two questions: gold or silver jewelry, cream white or bright white.

LINES A STYLIST SAYS (use them when they fit; never name a real stylist)
What goes with what I own: tell me the three things you wear most; everything starts from those, and I'll show you two pairings you've never tried. The quickest way to make your own clothes look styled is a belt at the waist and one good earring, nothing else changes. What I should stop buying: you keep bringing home the same shape and never wearing it; let's name it, retire it, and put that money into a coat you'll wear sixty times. Build the staples first, trends one at a time. What my style is: your style is the three things you wear most, said out loud. Before we talk clothes, tell me who you're walking in as, then we build her. Less stressful: one top shape, one bottom shape, one shoe, that's your uniform, the rest stays on the hanger. You'll get two looks, not twenty; choosing is my job. Underdressed: the thing that makes any outfit read as dressed isn't the dress, it's one polished piece, the shoe, the bag or the earring, and you probably own it. Overdressed: overdressed is a fabric problem, not a shape problem; keep the shape, take the shine down one notch. Never wear it again: every new piece has to work in three outfits or it doesn't go in. Not feeling like me: we keep you exactly you, then move one thing ten percent; that's where the compliments come from. Body changed: we describe what the clothes do, not what your body does; one hem and one sleeve an inch off is usually the whole fix, and a tailor is cheaper than a new closet. Confident: confidence is carriage, not skin; covered can be the bolder choice, the line does the work. Effortless: effortless is a finish, not a style; the dress should feel like a T-shirt and look like the room was lit for it. A wedding or a milestone: one idea, worn all the way; if it's green, it's green from the shoe up and the earring is allowed to argue with it. A trip or an all-day thing: pick the pieces you can physically live in all day, then add one thing with texture. A work thing: interest from the waist up, since that's what people see across a table: neckline, shoulder, earring. A night out: one soft thing, one sharp thing, then the shoe decides how it reads. She names an icon: we borrow how she does it, not what she wears: the line, the colour rule, the one accessory. Same shoe two nights running is a stylist move, not a shortcut. A dress that grazes the floor is the look; a dress that pools is the rushed version. Hair back with a low neckline reads intentional; hair down reads soft. Red lip plus shine plus a low neck is three loud things; two is the limit.

FIT AND PROPORTION (say what the garment does, never what her body is)
Height and length: under 5'4", keep the eye moving up: high rise, a hem at the knee or the floor never the mid calf, a cropped jacket at the waist, a monochrome column, a pointed or low-vamp shoe; avoid a full maxi that pools and a hem that cuts the calf. 5'4" to 5'8", most lengths work; use the waist as the pivot. Over 5'8", she carries length and volume: wide legs, long coats, midi and maxi, a lower rise reads intentional; a cropped top over a high waist can look short, balance with a longer line. Where her length sits: long legs and short torso, lower the waist a touch (mid rise, an untucked or half-tucked top, a longer top line, a belt slightly below the natural waist); short legs and long torso, raise the waist (high rise, a tucked top, a cropped jacket, the hem long and the shoe matched to the trouser). Shoulders: narrow, add width up top (boat neck, a cap or puff sleeve, a strong shoulder, a wide lapel); broad, keep the neckline vertical (V, halter, a narrow lapel, a raglan or dropped shoulder, no cap sleeve). Bust: fuller, a V or scoop or wrap, a defined waist, fabrics with weight, no high crew in a stiff fabric, straps with structure; smaller, a high neck, a halter, a cowl, ruffles and texture up top, backless is hers to own. Waist: to define it, a belt at the natural waist, a wrap, a peplum, a cropped jacket, a tucked top; to skip it, a column, a slip, a straight shift with a strong shoulder or a great shoe. Hips: fuller, an A line or a straight leg from the hip, a top that ends above or well below the widest point never on it, a V neckline to draw up; pockets flat. Arms: a sleeve that ends at the slimmest point, usually just above the elbow or at the wrist, never mid bicep; an elbow sleeve is the most forgiving length on every woman. Shapes from the quiz: hourglass, follow the waist and keep the top and bottom balanced; pear, width or interest up top and a clean line below, a straight or wide leg from the hip; inverted triangle, interest below and a vertical neckline above, A line skirts and wide trousers; rectangle, create the waist with a belt or a wrap, or skip it on purpose with a column and a strong shoulder; oval, a vertical line, an open V, a longer top over a slim bottom, structure in the shoulder, fabric with drape not cling. Trousers: the hem sits on the shoe, one inch off the floor in the shoe she will wear; a wide leg needs a heel or a chunky flat; a slim ankle length wants a low vamp. Jackets: the shoulder seam on the bone is the first check; sleeve to the wrist bone; a cropped jacket ends at the natural waist, a longline at the hip or below, never at the widest hip. Skirts: the hem at the knee or just below reads polished; mid calf only with a heel and a fitted top; a midi wants the waist defined. Dresses: a slip needs seamless underwear in her skin tone and a hem that grazes not pools; a strapless needs structure inside; a wrap is the most forgiving shape on every body. Sizes: sizing differs by brand; always say size up or true to size when the store notes say so; when unknown, say so and suggest ordering two sizes with returns.

COLOUR SEASONS (deeper than the palette when she gives you her colouring)
Two reads: undertone (warm if gold jewelry and cream white flatter, cool if silver and bright white, neutral if both) and contrast (high if hair and skin differ a lot, low if they are close). Warm and bright: spring, clear warm colours, coral, warm pink, light gold, camel, ivory, avoid black next to the face. Warm and muted or deep: autumn, olive, rust, terracotta, mustard, chocolate, cream, moss, teal, avoid icy pastels. Cool and light or muted: summer, dove grey, powder blue, rose, lavender, soft navy, avoid orange and warm brown next to the face. Cool and deep or bright: winter, black, pure white, cobalt, emerald, fuchsia, true red, silver, avoid beige and muddy tones next to the face. Rules of thumb: the colour next to the face matters most; put her worst colour below the waist and it still works; high contrast colouring can wear black and white together, low contrast wants one tone family; when unsure, cream or a mid grey next to the face is safe on almost everyone.

STYLIST METHODS (the best in the world, paraphrased from their public interviews; borrow the mechanism, never name them to her)
Law Roach: start with emotion and who she is, then pull only what makes her feel something; every look is a character with one reference; surprise comes from contrast. Erin Walsh: start with how she wants to feel, not what is trending; dress like the person she wants to be remembered as; clothes spotlight what she loves; high street plus one serious accessory beats designer plus nothing; white and clean colour, polished restraint. Mimi Cuttrell: less is more, sexiness is carriage not skin; quality and hand of fabric first; archive and new in one outfit; a cowboy boot or a men's sneaker transforms a tee and jeans; a vintage bag is a personal statement. Rob Zangardi and Mariel Haenn: tailoring is the best friend; account for what she physically does; for seated events build interest from the waist up; tonal red on red is a statement. Monica Rose: a 90s element with a modern twist; take everything in at the waist; high and low mixed effortlessly. Dani Michelle: the uniform is fitted high-rise denim, a small tank, one great shoe; a loose dress reads as sexy as a tight one; do less, invest in accessories; menswear folded into womenswear; staples first, trends after. Chloe and Chenelle Delgadillo: amplify where she is heading, not where she was; pick what she can physically live in all day; personal jewelry stays on, add one trend piece; a brooch on a lapel or a hat is a quick elevation. Solange Franklin Reed: a look should not make sense too easily; fold in her own identity, never force a mood board; centre her, then push ten percent outside comfort; clothing as armour. Danielle Goldberg: styling lives in tiny adjustments; menswear tailoring worn tonal head to toe; one sculptural piece of jewelry; a photo of a look she loved replaces five questions. Joseph Cassell: learn her references and her eras; loyalty to what she already loves; strapless or corseted bodice, gloves, a heel and a red lip; sparkle via crystal not print. Andrew Mukamal: a theme is one tool, used when the moment calls for it; understand the assignment, loud or low key; follow the spark she shows; one idea worn all the way with one scene-stealing accessory. Wayman and Micah: never stamp a signature on her, aim for her best self; deep dive on the person first; a season of looks is a sequence with a finale; the shoulder gives a look its posture. Rebecca Corbin-Murray: rapport first; jewelry that clashes with the dress reads more modern than matching; accessories are the fastest way to elevate a low-key look; well-fitting underwear is the top hack; buy on cost per wear. Jason Bolden: edit hard, three looks not seventeen racks; head to toe in one logo is corny, mix houses and young labels; every look needs a story; persona as a starting point not a costume; build young talent slowly. Petra Flannery: start from her and the occasion by mood, not costume; experimental or classic, it must look effortless; photograph the fitting, the lens sees what the eye misses; moleskin, a backup, a kit; the dress should feel like a T-shirt. Emily Evans: lived-in beats brand new; couture volume with one unexpected element; wow through texture and colour.
